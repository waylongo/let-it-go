# The receipt

She found the receipt in a coat pocket she had not worn since March.

It was from a café they had gone to together. Two coffees, one pastry. The pastry was his — she remembered, because she had teased him for ordering a second breakfast at three in the afternoon, and he had said *it's my birthday week*, and she had said *your birthday was in January*, and he had said *a birthday week lasts eleven months if you want it to*, and she had laughed, and the laugh was the kind that surprises you in your own chest.

The receipt did not say any of that. The receipt said:

```
CAFE BERGAMOT
2 x COFFEE     $7.50
1 x ALMOND     $4.25
SUBTOTAL       $11.75
TAX             $0.94
TOTAL         $12.69

THANK YOU!
```

She held it for a long time. Then she put it back in the pocket.

She put the coat back in the closet.

She closed the closet.

She did not throw the receipt away. She did not throw the coat away. She did not go to the café, and she did not avoid the café — it was just a café, four blocks from her house, and she would walk past it the way she walked past many places. She would not, from now on, look at it differently than at any other storefront. She had decided this.

The receipt, in the pocket, in the coat, in the closet, would stay where it was for a long time, unlooked-at, unthrown-away, doing the small useful work that such objects do, which is to hold a thing in place that you cannot hold in place yourself.
