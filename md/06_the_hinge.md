# The hinge

Consider, for a second, the hinge on a cheap cabinet door.

It is the kind of object that was designed once, a hundred years ago, by someone whose name no one remembers, and then manufactured a trillion times by people who never thought about it at all. It costs less than a cup of coffee. It weighs less than a sandwich. If you have a house, you probably own forty of them. If you have ever opened a kitchen cabinet, you have trusted your fingers to one, and you did not notice doing it.

The hinge has to do something strange: let the door move freely in one direction and absolutely refuse to let it move in any other. It must accept rotation and reject translation. It must do this ten thousand times without lubrication, without inspection, without thanks. It must do it when the wood has swelled in August humidity and when the wood has shrunk in February cold. It must do it when a small child hangs on the door with full body weight, which the designer accounted for, somehow, a century ago.

And here is the part that gets me. The hinge does not know it is succeeding. There is no feedback loop. No counter ticking up with each successful swing. The hinge performs its duty in total indifference to whether anyone appreciates it, and if it ever does fail — a screw backs out, a pin wears through, the geometry drifts — the blame goes to the door, or to the cabinet, or to whoever installed it. The hinge, which did more than any other component to make the cabinet a cabinet at all, is the last thing anyone examines.

I think about this when I think about the parts of a life that go unthanked. The municipal water pressure. The fact that the power comes on. The particular molecule in your knee that has withstood four decades of stairs. The shopkeeper who restocked the shelf at 4 a.m. so that the cereal you wanted was there at 9 a.m.. None of this announces itself. All of it is load-bearing.

A life is mostly held up by hinges. The failure of a hinge is always loud. The success of a hinge is always silent. If you want to practice gratitude, don't start with the dramatic things. Start with the cabinet door that opened smoothly this morning, and try to hold, for just one second, the fact that someone had to think of that.
