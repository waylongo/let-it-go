# The bulb

The lightbulb in the hallway went out on a Wednesday, and Marta decided to ignore it.

This was not a decision she made once. It was a decision she made many times, each evening when she came home and the hall stayed dark, each morning when she walked through it carrying her coffee. The first few times she thought: *I should replace that.* After a week she stopped thinking it. After two weeks the dark hallway had become a feature of the apartment, like the radiator that clicked or the floorboard that creaked near the bathroom. She knew where the furniture was. She did not need the light.

Her daughter came to visit on a Sunday and walked straight into the hall table.

"Mom."

"I know."

"Mom, why is it dark in here."

"The bulb went."

"When."

Marta considered. "A while ago."

Her daughter looked at her with the particular expression daughters reserve for mothers who have begun to disappear in small ways. Marta recognized the look. She had used it herself on her own mother, twenty years ago, over things that had seemed, at the time, like warnings. Now she was on the other side of it, and she saw what her mother must have seen: that the look was a form of love, and also a form of judgment, and that the two were harder to separate than either party wanted to admit.

"I'll get you a bulb," her daughter said.

"I have bulbs. In the drawer."

"Then why—"

"I just haven't."

The daughter went to the drawer and found the bulbs, and got the stepladder from the closet, and changed the bulb. The hallway filled with the familiar yellow light, and Marta saw, for the first time in weeks, the small painting her husband had hung there in 1987, a watercolor of a harbor that neither of them had ever visited. It was still on the wall. She had walked past it, in the dark, every day, without knowing if it was still there. She had not thought to wonder.

Her daughter came down from the ladder and folded it and looked at her mother.

"Mom."

"I see it," Marta said. "I see."

What she meant was: *I see the painting, I see that I stopped seeing it, I see why you are looking at me that way, I see that this will happen again and that next time it might not be a bulb, I see, I see.* But what she said was *I see it*, and her daughter, who was a kind person, decided that this was enough, and did not ask her to say more.
