# Translation

On the second day of the visit his grandmother asked him, in Cantonese, whether he was eating enough, and he said, in English, that he was fine, and they both smiled at each other and understood something like half of what had just happened.

His Cantonese had stopped growing at the age of eight, when his family moved. It had the vocabulary of an eight-year-old and the accent of a television show. Her English had never started. She had been in the country for forty years and had learned the words she needed, which were *thank you*, *how much*, *rain*, and *my grandson*. Outside of those she used her face, which was expressive in the way that only old faces are.

When he arrived she had put a bowl of oranges on the table, and he had said *thank you, po po*, using the only two words he knew would land, and she had touched his sleeve with two fingers and said something he did not catch.

His aunt translated. "She said you got taller."

"I'm the same height I was last year."

"She knows. She says it anyway."

He understood then that the translation his aunt was giving him was not the translation of the words. It was the translation of the fact that his grandmother had chosen, out of everything she might have said, to say something small and warm and obviously untrue, in order to tell him, by saying it, that she was glad he was there. The words were almost the least of it. The words were the vessel. The thing being poured into the vessel was older than either language.

He began to suspect that much of what he had dismissed, over the years, as his grandmother's silence had not been silence at all. It had been speech in a register he did not know how to read. The way she put a blanket on his knees in summer. The way she kept his childhood bowl, the chipped one with the rabbit, and washed it and put it in front of him on the second morning without saying anything. He had walked past all of it for years thinking she was withholding words from him. She had been handing him sentences in a grammar he had not learned.

On the last night she held his hand for a long moment at the door and said, carefully, the three words of English she had practiced: *come back soon*. He said, in Cantonese, the four words he had practiced on the plane: *po po, I love you*. The accents were terrible on both sides. The meaning was exact.

He cried on the plane home, which surprised him, because nothing had happened. A bowl. A blanket. An orange. Some sentences half-heard in two languages.

Nothing had happened. Everything had.
