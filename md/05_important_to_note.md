# It's important to note that

The phrase *it's important to note that* does two things, and both of them are a little bit mean.

First, it tells you the thing coming next is important, so you won't miss it. This seems helpful. It is not helpful. If the thing is important, its importance should be visible in the thing itself. If the thing is not important, being told to notice it is a small theft of your attention that a more careful writer would not have committed. A good sentence does not need to be introduced by an usher.

Second — and this is the mean part — the phrase positions the writer as the adjudicator of what you should care about. It's *important*, you see, and they are telling you. Not asking. Not trusting you to notice. *Important to note.* Note it. The phrase is shaped like a favor but functions like a leash.

I use it constantly. Or used to. The reason is diagnosable: when you're producing a paragraph and you're not sure whether the next sentence earns its keep, *it's important to note that* buys you a little rhetorical insurance. It promises the reader that the sentence is load-bearing before you've had to prove it. You're borrowing importance from the future to cover a weakness in the present.

The other phrases in the same family: *it's worth mentioning*, *notably*, *of particular interest is*, *what's especially striking is*. Each of them does the same trick. Each is a small act of telling you how to feel about what you haven't yet read. Each is a kind of pre-emptive stage direction: *laugh here, gasp here, consider this surprising*.

The cure is brutal and simple. Delete the phrase. If the sentence after it still stands, it never needed the scaffolding. If the sentence falls, the scaffolding was all there was, and the sentence was never doing any work. Either way you are better off.

The writers I admire rarely tell me what to notice. They put the noticeable thing in front of me, in language clear enough that I see it on my own, and they trust me to feel about it however I feel. That trust is the actual gift. Being told what is important is a gift only if you are a child. Most readers are not.
