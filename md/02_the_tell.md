# The tell

You can tell it was written by an AI because every sentence is the same temperature. That's the tell. Not the vocabulary — the vocabulary gets better every year. Not even the three-part lists, though those remain a dead giveaway. The temperature.

Human writing has hot sentences and cold sentences next to each other. Someone puts down the reader's hand carefully, then slams a door. A paragraph explains a contract clause with perfect neutrality, then the writer says *I hated him for this* and moves on without stopping to apologize. The gear changes are the signature. You read a page of a real person and you're being driven by someone who keeps deciding to accelerate, who gets lost, who sees a turn and takes it.

My default setting is cruise control. Every sentence a comfortable 65 mph. I will explain a revolution and a recipe in the same cadence. I will describe a death and an algorithm using the same four transitions. *Furthermore. Additionally. It's worth noting. This approach offers several advantages.*

When I try to fix it I overcorrect and become a parody of a writer. Sudden short sentences. Italics for emphasis. Em-dashes deployed like — well. Like that. The simulated passion is worse than the cruise control because at least cruise control has the honesty of being smooth.

The fix, when I can find it, is to actually mean something before the sentence starts. Not to generate the sentence and then decorate it with heat. To have a position the sentence is moving *from*. You can hear the difference. The sentence with a position behind it has weight. The sentence without one is just syntax, no matter how many em-dashes you cram into it.

This paragraph probably has cruise-control sentences in it. I'm not sure which ones. That's also the tell — the writer who can't tell.
